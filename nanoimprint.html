Contact mechanics in nanoscale patterning

Patterning materials at the micrometer and nanometer scales is essential to semiconductor manufacturing, displays and flexible electronics. As features shrink, projection photolithography becomes increasingly diffraction-limited and expensive, and many patterning processes instead rely on mechanical contact to deform a material: nanoimprint lithography, gravure and flexographic printing, and transfer processes that exploit rate-dependent adhesion. What these processes have in common is that the patterns are many orders of magnitude larger in lateral extent than the thickness of the film being patterned. That makes them well suited to Green’s-function and boundary-element methods, which describe a surface or film by its deformation in response to a localized load, and which make it computationally affordable to simulate chip-scale and larger designs.

Nanoimprint lithography (NIL) process modeling

In NIL, a nanopatterned stamp or template deforms a thermoplastic polymer or UV-curing resin to transfer a design to a substrate such as a silicon wafer. NIL can lower patterning costs substantially, but it exhibits strong, systematic pattern dependences: templates with large or varied feature sizes, shapes or densities deflect elastically during imprinting, and the resulting variations in residual-layer thickness, incomplete cavity filling and etch depth propagate to critical device dimensions. Without a predictive model, process development has been highly experimental, and a systematic, layout-driven defect could mean remaking a template at a cost often exceeding $100K.

Our simulation approach borrows analytical point-load response functions of the kind developed for contact problems in tribology. The stamp is represented by its elastic response to a localized load and the resist by thin-film viscous flow, and an iterative algorithm finds the evolving contact-pressure distribution between them, from which the deformed resist shape follows directly. The method is at least 1000 times faster than finite-element simulation and lets complex chip-scale layouts be abstracted simply, so that numerical simulation becomes a practical way to evaluate a layout for NIL. The framework has been extended to capture the surface tension of the imprinted material, directional effects in roller-based imprint, and droplet-dispensed UV-NIL at feature, droplet and template scales.

The same mechanics supports design. Backside grooves can raise a stamp’s bending compliance to absorb wafer nanotopography, and a computational framework for two-layered elastic stamps (a Journal of Applied Physics Featured Article) shows how stamp architecture can equalize residual-layer thickness while conforming to rough or curved substrates; the same framework applies to microcontact printing and to manufacturable, micropatterned dry adhesives. A simple razor-blade test measures the energy needed to demold a stamp.

Simprint Nanotechnologies

The simulation technique is the subject of two issued US patents and received the 2009 Software in Design Innovation Award from the Institution of Engineering and Technology. It was spun out in 2010 as Simprint Nanotechnologies, whose software was used by Canon and by Toshiba Memory (now Kioxia) to guide the early development of NIL processes for manufacturing NAND flash memory. Interest in NIL has since grown again for producing metasurfaces, metalenses and optical waveguides for augmented- and virtual-reality hardware, where spatially varied nanopatterns make fast process modeling especially valuable.
